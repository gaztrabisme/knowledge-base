# Edge Model Compression & Energy Optimization for Jetson

This synthesis targets an on-orbit Earth-observation (EO) pipeline — a cloud-cover classifier acting as a gate, plus a land-cover segmentation network — running on Jetson-class hardware spanning the original Jetson Nano (Maxwell, ~0.5 FP16 TFLOPS, 4 GB, no DLA) to the Orin Nano (~40 INT8 TOPS, 8 GB, one DLA core). The governing constraint is **Joules per useful inference**, not raw accuracy or even latency. Every technique below is judged on whether it actually reduces energy on *real* Jetson silicon — and several popular techniques do not.

A unifying frame: model compression is a multiplier on a per-frame energy cost, but the largest mission-energy lever is *not running the segmentation net at all* on frames the cloud-gate rejects. The compression recipe and the duty-cycle architecture must be designed together.

## Quantization (the single highest-value technique)

**What it does / mechanism.** Quantization reduces the numerical precision of weights and activations — FP32 → FP16 → INT8 (and below). Memory footprint falls linearly with bit-width (a model in 16-bit needs half the bytes of 32-bit; INT8 is a 4× reduction over FP32), and integer arithmetic units are both faster and far lower-energy per MAC than floating-point. The KB frames this directly: "Reducing precision, also known as quantization, is a cheap and extremely effective way to reduce a model's memory footprint… straightforward to do and generalizes over tasks and architectures" (`ai-engineering-huyen`, Ch7). Lower precision means less data moved per inference, which on a memory-bandwidth-bound edge device is the dominant energy term ([NVIDIA TensorRT DLA docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt/latest/inference-library/work-with-dla.html)).

**FP16 vs INT8.** FP16 is nearly free accuracy-wise and is the safe default; on the original Nano (Maxwell, no INT8 tensor path and no DLA) FP16 is effectively the floor. INT8 roughly doubles throughput and halves energy again versus FP16 on Orin, but only pays off when the hardware has INT8 units and the layers map to them — see the honest caveats below.

**PTQ vs QAT.** Post-training quantization (PTQ) calibrates scales from a small unlabeled calibration set after training — cheap, no retraining. Quantization-aware training (QAT) inserts fake-quant nodes into the training graph (quantize-then-dequantize forward, straight-through-estimator gradient) so the network *learns* to tolerate INT8; scales become learned/adapted ([NVIDIA QAT blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/)). Concrete gap on EfficientNet-B0: FP32 Top-1 77.4 → **PTQ collapses to 33.9** → **QAT recovers 76.8** ([NVIDIA QAT blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/)). The lesson: lightweight backbones (MobileNet/EfficientNet/ShuffleNet — exactly the ones you want on a satellite) are the ones PTQ damages most ([Efficient Embedded DL survey, arXiv 2411.01431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01431)).

**Segmentation vs classification.** Segmentation is *more* sensitive (dense per-pixel output, boundary precision), but QAT still keeps it within ~1 mIoU. TensorFlow Model Garden measured PTQ at **−1.3 mIoU** vs FP32, QAT at **−0.7 mIoU**, at comparable latency — i.e., QAT roughly halves the segmentation accuracy loss ([TensorFlow blog](https://blog.tensorflow.org/2022/06/Adding-Quantization-aware-Training-and-Pruning-to-the-TensorFlow-Model-Garden.html)). General guidance: expect ~0.5% Top-1 drop for classification and <1 mIoU for segmentation under QAT ([TensorFlow blog](https://blog.tensorflow.org/2022/06/Adding-Quantization-aware-Training-and-Pruning-to-the-TensorFlow-Model-Garden.html)).

**Per-tensor vs per-channel; calibration pitfalls.** Per-channel (per-output-channel) quantization keeps a separate scale per conv filter and substantially reduces error versus a single per-tensor scale — essential for depthwise-separable layers in mobile backbones; it requires ONNX opset ≥13 ([NVIDIA QAT blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/)). Calibration pitfalls: `max` calibration is destroyed by outlier activations, so prefer histogram/entropy (KLD) or percentile calibrators; and the calibration set must be representative — for EO, calibrate on the actual orbit/sensor distribution (cloud, ocean, desert, night) or INT8 scales will be miscalibrated for whole scene classes.

**Honest caveat.** INT8 is not a guaranteed win. A documented case shows TensorRT INT8 causing a **2.7× *slowdown*** on Orin Nano 4 GB for a ViT-S + DPT (transformer) model ([NVIDIA Forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/tensorrt-model-optimizer-int8-quantization-causes-2-7x-performance-regression-on-jetson-orin-nano-4gb-vit-s-dpt-architecture/357835)) — transformers don't map to INT8 kernels or the DLA well. Keep the backbone convolutional.

## Pruning (structured helps on Jetson; unstructured usually does not)

**What it does / mechanism.** Pruning removes parameters deemed least useful. Two granularities, and the distinction is decisive for real speedup:

- **Unstructured** zeros individual weights. Theoretically removes up to ~90% of weights with little accuracy loss, but produces irregular sparse matrices. Standard libraries run dense GEMM regardless of zeros; realizing the sparsity needs CSR-style indexing that "is generally not compatible with GPU acceleration… speed degradation can often be observed" ([Embedded DL survey, arXiv 2411.01431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01431); [APXML](https://apxml.com/courses/llm-compression-acceleration/chapter-3-sophisticated-pruning-methodologies/unstructured-vs-structured-pruning)). **On Jetson, unstructured pruning is mostly a disappointment** — smaller files, not faster or lower-energy inference.
- **Structured** removes whole channels/filters/layers, yielding a smaller *dense* network that GPUs and the DLA accelerate directly with no special kernels; observed speedup tracks the FLOP/param reduction ([Embedded DL survey, arXiv 2411.01431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01431)). Cost: coarser granularity hurts accuracy more at a given sparsity, so prune iteratively with fine-tuning between rounds.

**Semi-structured (2:4 / N:M).** The middle ground: two of every four contiguous weights are zero. Orin's DLA 2.0 supports 2:4 structured sparsity in hardware, giving real acceleration for INT8 convolutions that are math-bound with channel counts that are multiples of 128 ([NVIDIA DLA docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt/latest/inference-library/work-with-dla.html)); on GPU sparse tensor cores this is ~1.3–1.6× on transformer blocks ([Embedded DL survey, arXiv 2411.01431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.01431)).

**Lottery ticket.** The lottery-ticket hypothesis says a dense net contains a sparse subnetwork that, trained from the original init, matches full accuracy — intellectually important and a good way to *find* what to prune, but the winning tickets are unstructured, so they inherit the no-speedup problem on commodity hardware unless re-cast to channel-level sparsity ([Chase / NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/d6d0e41e0b1ed38c76d13c9e417a8f1f-Paper-Conference.pdf), which converts unstructured to channel sparsity for a real **1.5×** GPU speedup). The KB notes the broader caveat that the common form of pruning "doesn't reduce the total number of parameters, only the number of nonzero parameters" (`designing-ml-systems-huyen`, Ch7) — exactly why it fails to accelerate dense hardware.

**Verdict for the satellite:** use **structured channel pruning**, iterative, with fine-tuning; treat unstructured/lottery-ticket as research tools, not deployment levers.

## Knowledge distillation (high value, and it fits your SAM3-teacher plan)

**What it does / mechanism.** A small **student** is trained to mimic a large **teacher**. The student learns from soft targets (teacher's softened logits via KL divergence) and/or intermediate features, which carry more information than one-hot labels (`designing-ml-systems-huyen`, Ch7; `ai-engineering-huyen`, Ch6). Two families:

- **Logit distillation** — match softened output distributions with KL divergence. Simple, cheap, architecture-agnostic, but misses internal structure ([Sci. Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-82647-6)).
- **Feature distillation** (FitNet-style hints) — align intermediate feature maps via L2, plus attention-transfer variants. Richer supervision, faster student convergence, but imposes architectural alignment constraints ([AICSD, arXiv 2308.04243](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04243)).

**Realistic gains.** The canonical production result: **DistilBERT is 40% smaller, retains 97% of capability, and runs ~60% faster** (`designing-ml-systems-huyen`, Ch7). For dense vision, distillation can be dramatic — a crack-segmentation study with HRNet reported **up to +7.5% average IoU** for the student, with cases where the student *exceeds* the teacher (it learns ground truth plus teacher knowledge, reducing overfit) ([crack-seg, arXiv 2312.04233](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04233)). For segmentation, the student logits must be spatially aligned to the teacher's (interpolate/conv if resolutions differ) ([AICSD, arXiv 2308.04243](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04243)).

**Relevance to "SAM3-teacher → small student."** This is the right pattern: use the large foundation segmenter (SAM-family) as a teacher to auto-label EO imagery and/or to supervise a compact CNN student via soft labels and feature hints. Recent work specifically distills **vision foundation models into compact segmentation students**, transferring out-of-domain robustness through a query-based soft-distillation mechanism — directly applicable to varied orbital scenes ([Generalizable KD from VFMs, arXiv 2603.02554](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02554)). Distillation also generates training data cheaply, which matters because labeled EO segmentation masks are scarce.

## TensorRT + the Jetson stack (this is how compression becomes Joules)

**What it does / mechanism.** TensorRT parses a trained model and builds a hardware-specific engine: it quantizes to INT8/FP16, **fuses layers** into single kernels (cutting memory traffic and kernel-launch overhead), selects optimal data layouts, and auto-tunes the fastest kernel per layer for the *specific* Jetson SKU ([NVIDIA brief](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/gtcf21/jetson-orin/nvidia-jetson-agx-orin-technical-brief.pdf)). With explicit quantization (Q/DQ nodes from QAT), `trtexec -int8 model.onnx` auto-enables INT8 and preserves the trained scales ([NVIDIA QAT blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/achieving-fp32-accuracy-for-int8-inference-using-quantization-aware-training-with-tensorrt/)).

**Measured speedups.** TensorRT INT8 engines run **~10× faster than PyTorch** on AGX Orin, up to **~14.9× for MobileNet/SqueezeNet** ([TX State, MCSoC'23](https://userweb.cs.txstate.edu/~k_y47/webpage/pubs/mcsoc23.pdf)); YOLOv8 QAT INT8 gives **~2×** on Orin Nano, maximized by pushing as many layers as possible into INT8 ([Medium / Maro JEON](https://medium.com/@MaroJEON/quantization-yolov8-qat-x2-speed-up-on-your-jetson-orin-nano-2-how-to-achieve-the-best-qat-c6069fb83ab7)).

**DLA offload — the energy story.** The Orin Nano's single DLA core is a fixed-function CNN accelerator that is **on average 3–5× more power-efficient than the GPU**, saving roughly **8–15 W system-wide** at 30 fps on a well-quantized INT8 CNN ([NVIDIA DLA docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/tensorrt/latest/inference-library/work-with-dla.html); [ProventusNova](https://proventusnova.com/blog/tensorrt-vs-dla-jetson-orin/)). In constrained power modes the DLA carries an increasing share of total inference — **46% at 30 W, 74% at 15 W** on AGX Orin ([NVIDIA brief](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/gtcf21/jetson-orin/nvidia-jetson-agx-orin-technical-brief.pdf)) — which is exactly the regime a power-starved satellite lives in. Build with `trtexec --useDLACore=0 --allowGPUFallback --int8` and read the build log for DLA-vs-GPU layer assignment.

**The honest tradeoff.** DLA trades latency for energy: YOLOv8m runs **<5 ms on GPU INT8** but **15–25 ms on DLA** ([ProventusNova](https://proventusnova.com/blog/tensorrt-vs-dla-jetson-orin/)). For a low-duty-cycle EO payload that is *energy*-bound, not throughput-bound, this trade is favorable. Caveats: transformers/attention, dynamic shapes, and custom plugins **fall back to GPU**, which erases the power benefit and adds transfer overhead — so the on-board nets must be plain CNNs. Engines are **not portable across Jetson SKUs**; build on the target device.

## Hardware-aware efficient backbones & NAS (choose the right net before compressing it)

Compression of a bloated backbone is dominated by simply *starting* from an efficient one.

- **MobileNetV3** — built by hardware-aware NAS (MnasNet-style, latency in the search objective) + NetAdapt, with depthwise-separable convs, squeeze-excite, and hard-swish. MobileNetV3-Large hits **75.2% ImageNet Top-1** while being **3.2% more accurate and 15% lower latency** than V2; the Small variant (2.5M params) targets the tightest budgets ([Searching for MobileNetV3, arXiv 1905.02244](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.02244)). A strong default backbone for both the cloud-gate and the segmentation encoder.
- **EfficientNet-Lite** — EfficientNet stripped of squeeze-excite and swish (which quantize poorly), making it INT8/edge-friendly; pairs well with the QAT path above.
- **MCUNet (TinyNAS + TinyEngine)** — co-designs the architecture *and* the inference engine for severe memory limits: **>70% ImageNet Top-1 on a microcontroller**, with **3.5× less SRAM / 5.7× less Flash** than quantized MobileNetV2, and TinyEngine giving **4.8× less memory and 1.7–3.3× faster** inference than TF-Lite Micro/CMSIS-NN ([MCUNet, arXiv 2007.10319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10319)). Directly relevant if any payload must run on a microcontroller-class co-processor below the Jetson.
- **Once-for-All (OFA)** — trains one weight-shared supernet, then extracts specialized subnets per deployment target *without retraining*. Ideal when one trained model must be specialized across the Nano→Orin hardware spread ([Han et al.; OFA latency-target work, arXiv 2012.06748](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.06748)). This is the MIT EfficientML (Song Han) lineage — MnasNet → ProxylessNAS → OFA → MCUNet — whose through-line is *putting the hardware cost function inside the search loop*.

## The accuracy-vs-energy/latency tradeoff (think in Joules and duty cycles)

**Energy per inference is the metric**, integrating power × time, measured in mJ/µJ — instantaneous Watts alone mislead ([Power-mgmt for edge AI](https://dev.to/beefedai/power-management-strategies-for-battery-powered-edge-ai-devices-3gef)). On CubeSat-class hardware, optimized EO models land in the **sub-mJ to single-mJ** range: a closely-analogous study (EuroSAT/MEDIC/RS_C11 on an STM32N6 Cortex-M55 + NPU) reports **0.68–6.45 mJ/inference, 3.22–30.38 ms latency**, with an optimization pipeline of *structured iterative pruning + PTQ INT8 + hardware-aware operator mapping*, achieving **89.6% RAM and 70.1% Flash reduction** at an accuracy cost of only **0.4–8.6 pp** vs FP32 ([TinyML for CubeSat, arXiv 2603.20174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20174)). That is essentially a blueprint for your stack.

**The mission-level lever is duty cycling + on-board filtering.** Inference energy is typically *negligible next to the radio*. A representative system measured **>50× higher** energy for transmit-everything vs infer-then-standby ([capsule edge-AI, arXiv 2601.17752](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17752)); the standing advice is "tiny inference energy becomes irrelevant if your radio transmits too frequently" ([Power-mgmt for edge AI](https://dev.to/beefedai/power-management-strategies-for-battery-powered-edge-ai-devices-3gef)). For EO this is decisive: ~50–70% of orbital frames are cloud-obscured. A cheap cloud-gate (a few mJ) that **drops cloudy frames before they ever hit the segmentation net or the downlink** saves both the segmentation compute *and* the dominant downlink energy — the single biggest mission-energy win, larger than any compression ratio on the segmentation net itself. Duty-cycle the GPU/DLA: keep them gated off, wake on a scheduled capture, run the gate, and only spin up segmentation + downlink on frames that pass.

## Recommended compression recipe (cloud-gate + land-cover seg on Jetson)

Concrete, ordered pipeline. The cloud-gate's job is to be cheap and to prevent work; the segmentation net is compressed hard because it is the expensive stage that only runs on accepted frames.

1. **Architect the cascade, not just the models.** Stage 1 = tiny cloud-cover classifier as an energy gate; Stage 2 = land-cover segmentation, invoked only on low-cloud frames. Budget energy per *captured frame*, dominated by Stage 1 since most frames are rejected.
2. **Pick efficient backbones up front.** Cloud-gate: MobileNetV3-Small or an MCUNet-class net (could even run on a microcontroller co-processor). Segmentation: MobileNetV3-Large or EfficientNet-Lite encoder (avoid transformers — they break DLA/INT8). On the original Nano, target FP16 only.
3. **Distill the segmentation student from a SAM3 teacher.** Use SAM3 to auto-label EO imagery and supervise the compact student with logit + feature (FitNet/attention) distillation; spatially align logits. Expect the student to approach teacher mIoU at a fraction of the cost ([crack-seg +7.5% IoU](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04233); [VFM→compact KD](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02554)).
4. **Structured channel pruning, iterative.** Prune filters with fine-tuning between rounds; skip unstructured pruning (no Jetson speedup). Where Orin DLA is the target, add **2:4 structured sparsity** on INT8 convs with channel multiples of 128.
5. **QAT to INT8 with per-channel scales.** Train with fake-quant so the student tolerates INT8 (PTQ collapses lightweight backbones); use histogram/percentile calibration on a representative multi-scene EO set. Budget ~10% of the training schedule for QAT fine-tuning. Keep FP16 fallback for any layer that loses too much mIoU. Expect <1 mIoU loss for segmentation, ~0.5% for the gate.
6. **Build a TensorRT engine on the target device, offload CNN layers to DLA.** `trtexec --int8 --useDLACore=0 --allowGPUFallback`; verify the log shows the conv stack on DLA (3–5× more power-efficient, ~8–15 W saved) and accept the higher per-inference latency since the payload is energy- not throughput-bound. Build separate engines per Jetson SKU (consider OFA to specialize one trained supernet across Nano→Orin).
7. **Duty-cycle and filter on board.** Gate the GPU/DLA off between captures; wake, run the cloud-gate, and only invoke segmentation + downlink on accepted frames. This drop-cloudy-frames step saves more total mission energy (compute *and* the radio that dominates the budget) than any further squeezing of the segmentation net.
8. **Measure in Joules, then decide where to spend effort.** Instrument energy-per-inference (integrate power over a GPIO-gated window) and duty cycle separately; if the radio dominates, tighten the gate's reject rate before compressing the seg net further. Validate INT8 actually helps on your exact layers — it can regress (the 2.7× ViT slowdown) when ops don't map to INT8/DLA.
